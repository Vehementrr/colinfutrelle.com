Backcountry Skier

There's a particular kind of quiet at first light, skins stuck to the base of my skis, breath clouding in front of me, climbing toward a line nobody's skied yet that day. No lift line, no groomed run, no patrol sweeping behind me. Just the mountain, the snow, and whatever judgment I brought with me that morning.

Backcountry skiing doesn't forgive a lazy read of the terrain. Snow that looked stable an hour ago can wake up entirely different by afternoon, and the only way to ski it safely is to actually pay attention: to the slope angle, to how the snow sounds underfoot, to the small signs that something's changed. I've learned to trust that read, and just as importantly, to know when to turn around before the mountain makes the decision for me.

I never ski it alone, and never ski it recklessly. The partners I choose to ski with matter as much as the line we're skiing, because the plan only works if everyone on it is honest about what they're seeing.
